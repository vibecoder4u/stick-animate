Every morning, Mira opens her laptop to finish one important task. A message arrives, then another, and she switches between them. Even after returning to her document, part of her attention is still tied to the previous conversation; imagine a thin glowing thread stretching from her mind back to a half-open message window. By noon, Mira faces twelve possible ways to solve one simple problem and freezes. Picture a tiny person standing inside a maze made from branching doors, with paths that loop back into themselves. Later, she promises the report will take twenty minutes, but the calendar fills with unfinished steps. Show the plan as a fragile paper ladder trying to reach a clock mounted high on a wall. These patterns are attention residue, choice overload, and the planning fallacy. Once Mira can recognize them, she pauses, chooses one next action, and estimates the work more honestly.
